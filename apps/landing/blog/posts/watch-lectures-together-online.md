Recorded lectures have one big weakness: nothing stops you from putting them off until the night before the exam. An online study group fixes that. When three or four people agree to watch lectures together at a set time, the lecture actually gets watched, and the confusing parts get talked through while they're still fresh.

This guide shows how to run that kind of session online, with everyone's video in sync, a simple rule for questions, and a plan that fits into an evening.

## Why watch lectures together

Studying alone with a recording is efficient right up until you hit something you don't understand. Then you either rewind it five times or skip it and hope it isn't on the exam.

With a group, the person who got it explains it to the person who didn't. Explaining is one of the better ways to check your own understanding, so both of them come out ahead. And a fixed session time with friends is harder to cancel than a vague plan to "watch week 6 at some point."

## What you need

Each person needs Chrome on a laptop or desktop and a Google account to sign in. Watchbear runs as a Chrome extension, so phones and tablets are out for this one.

You also need a lecture that everyone can open. That covers:

- Lectures posted on YouTube or Vimeo, which many universities and course creators use.
- Course platforms that play video in a standard player, as long as every member of the group has their own login.
- Conference talks, coding tutorials and other long-form videos you're studying from.

Some course platforms use custom video players, and support for those varies by site. Try a short session before the night you really need it.

## How to set up a study session

1. Agree on the lecture a day ahead and post the link in your group chat, along with a start time.
2. Everyone installs [Watchbear from the Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/watchbear-watch-together/ldegfikaldilbcpgmiopdnnhpkpcnepn) if they haven't already.
3. The host opens the lecture page and clicks the bear icon. The side panel opens with a room ready.
4. The host copies the invite link and sends it to the group. It opens the same lecture and puts each person in the room.
5. Turn on voice chat. Up to 8 people can be on voice at once, which covers most study groups, and speaking indicators show who's talking.
6. Press play. From here, anyone can play, pause or skip, and everyone's video follows.

## The pause-to-ask rule

The single most useful habit for a group study session: anyone can pause at any time, and a pause means "I have a question." Because a pause from one person pauses every screen, nobody has to interrupt by talking over the lecturer. You stop, talk it through, and carry on.

Skipping works the same way. If a derivation went by too fast, someone skips back thirty seconds and the whole group rewatches it together.

> **Tip:** when something confuses you but you don't want to stop the lecture yet, type the timestamp in the chat, like "34:10 lost me." At the next natural break, go back through the list.

## A session plan that works

A 50-minute lecture usually turns into a 75 to 90 minute session once you add pauses and discussion. One way to structure it:

### Five minutes to start

Say what you want to get out of the lecture. "Understand how the proof works" is a better goal than "finish the video."

### Watch in chunks

Play 15 to 20 minutes, then pause for a few minutes. One person sums up what was just covered in their own words, and the others fill in gaps.

### Ten minutes at the end

List the questions nobody could answer. Those go to the teaching assistant, the course forum or office hours.

### Rotate the roles

Sessions run more smoothly with a few jobs handed out, and rotating them keeps it fair:

- A note-taker who writes into a shared document everyone can see.
- A timekeeper who calls the breaks and keeps the session from drifting.
- A question collector who watches the chat for timestamps and brings them up at each break.

## Cameras, focus and other small things

Some groups find that cameras keep everyone honest, since it's harder to scroll your phone when people can see you. Watchbear has video chat built in, with tiles in the side panel beside the lecture rather than over the slides. Cameras start off every time you join, so each person decides whether to turn theirs on.

Close the other tabs before you start. The lecture tab is the one that stays in sync, and a cluttered browser makes it easy to wander off.

If you're using laptop speakers, stay muted while the lecture plays, or the microphone will pick up the lecturer's voice and echo it back to everyone.
