If you've tried to screen share a movie on Discord or Zoom, you know how it goes. The opening scene looks fine. Then the camera pans and the picture smears into blocks, the sound slips a beat behind the actors, and someone in the call says "it's frozen for me." On some streaming services the video turns into a black rectangle the moment you start sharing.

None of that means you set it up wrong. Screen sharing was built to show a slide deck or a spreadsheet to a meeting, and a film is close to the worst case for it.

## Why screen sharing a movie looks bad

### Calls are tuned for faces and slides

A video call has to squeeze everything into a stream that works on ordinary home connections. Slides and documents compress well because most of the screen stays still. A film changes almost every pixel many times a second, so the call throws away detail to keep up. Dark scenes turn muddy, fast action turns into blocks, and fine detail like rain or text on screen disappears.

Discord and Zoom both have settings that help a little. Zoom has an option to optimize a screen share for video, and Discord offers higher stream quality on its paid plan. Even at their best, though, viewers see a re-encoded copy of your screen rather than the film itself.

### The host's connection carries everyone

With a screen share, one person's upload speed decides what the whole group sees. Home connections usually upload far slower than they download. If the host's line has a bad moment, everyone's picture drops at once, and the host's own computer is busy playing the film and encoding the stream at the same time.

### The sound drifts or goes missing

Sharing audio along with the screen is a separate setting in most apps, and it doesn't always work the same way on every system. When it does work, the sound can arrive a little out of step with the picture. If the host is also talking on the call, their microphone can pick up the film and send it back a second time.

### Only the host can pause

Everyone else is watching a live feed of one person's screen. Nobody else can pause for a snack, rewind a line they missed, or turn the subtitles on just for themselves. The host ends up as the projectionist for the night.

## Why Netflix shows a black screen when you share it

Netflix and many other streaming services protect their video with DRM, a form of copy protection. In many browsers and setups, that protection stops screen capture tools from grabbing the picture. The people on your call see a black screen, sometimes with the sound still playing.

This is the streaming service working as intended, and getting around it can break its terms of use. The simpler route is to skip the capture step entirely: if everyone has their own account, everyone can play the film directly.

## What to use instead: a watch party extension

A watch party extension takes a different approach. Instead of sending your screen to your friends, it keeps everyone's own player in step. Each person opens the film on their own account in their own browser, at the full quality their connection can handle. The only thing that travels between you is the playback position: playing or paused, and at which second.

[Watchbear](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/watchbear-watch-together/ldegfikaldilbcpgmiopdnnhpkpcnepn) is a free Chrome extension that works this way. Setting it up takes about a minute:

1. Install Watchbear and open the film you want to watch.
2. Click the bear. A side panel opens next to the video with your room ready.
3. Send the invite link to your friends. It opens the same video for them and puts them in the room.
4. Press play. A play, pause or skip from anyone in the room moves everyone's player.

It works on YouTube, Vimeo, Netflix and most sites with a standard video player, and it has dedicated support for Netflix's player. Everyone does need their own access to whatever you're watching, since nobody's screen is being shared.

> **Keeping your Discord call?** That's fine. Use Discord for voice and Watchbear for the video. Just wear headphones, or your mic will pick up the film and everyone will hear it twice.

You may not need the separate call at all. Watchbear's side panel has chat and emoji reactions beside the video, plus built-in voice and video chat for up to 8 people at a time. Cameras start off every time you join, and the video tiles sit in the panel next to the film instead of on top of it. Calls go directly between the people in the room, and the quality adjusts as more people join.

## Screen sharing vs a watch party extension

With screen sharing, one person plays the film and everyone else gets a compressed copy of their screen. It works with nothing extra to install, but the picture suffers, the host's connection is the bottleneck, and only the host controls playback.

With a watch party extension, everyone plays the film themselves and only the timing is shared. Each person gets the full picture and sound, anyone can pause, and one slow connection doesn't drag the rest of the room down. The trade-off is that everyone needs the extension and their own access to the video.

For a two-hour film, the second option wins easily. If you're still seeing drift between players, our post on [why a watch party goes out of sync](why-watch-party-out-of-sync.html) covers the usual causes.

## When screen sharing is still fine

Screen sharing has its place. It's a good way to show a short clip during a meeting, walk someone through a game, or share a home video that only you have. For anything longer than a few minutes, or anything you'd actually like to see clearly, give everyone their own player.
